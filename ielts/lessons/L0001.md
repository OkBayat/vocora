# Food and everyday meals: form and meaning

- **Lesson ID:** L0001
- **Target level:** Foundation / elementary
- **CEFR:** Mainly A1 instruction, with supported A1–A2 reading
- **IELTS Band:** Approximate entry around 3.0; preparation towards 3–4 demands, not a predicted result
- **Primary skills:** Vocabulary-supported listening and reading
- **Supporting skills:** Speaking, writing, spelling and pronunciation awareness
- **Main language focus:** Food and drink meanings; subject–verb–object statements with I/you/we/they; present simple for regular meals; have breakfast; drink water
- **Prerequisites:** Recognise the English alphabet, enter short words and use audio playback. No earlier course lesson is required. The meanings of the target words, sentence roles and supporting expressions are taught here.

CEFR and IELTS describe proficiency in different ways. These are instructional labels, not an official conversion or a score awarded for finishing this lesson. See [IELTS and the CEFR](https://ielts.org/organisations/ielts-for-organisations/compare-ielts/ielts-and-the-cefr).

## Why this lesson exists

Knowing a translation is only a beginning. A learner may recognise *rice* on a card but fail to hear it, spell it or use it in a sentence. This lesson connects meaning, written form, sound and use through a familiar situation: an everyday meal. It begins with small messages so that the learner does not have to learn essay organisation and basic English at the same time.

By the end, the learner should be able to recognise and retrieve six words and two useful phrases, build simple statements, find a stated detail in a short text, follow which of two speakers eats or drinks something, and describe one real or invented meal. The learner also practises checking and revising their own message.

This is foundational English for IELTS Academic. It is not an IELTS mock test. A short meal description prepares for later familiar-topic Speaking practice and clear written communication; it is not an Academic Writing Task 1 response. Everyday topics are appropriate for the initial demands described in [official IELTS scoring guidance](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/your-results/ielts-scoring-in-detail).

## The eight lesson words and phrases

Learn each item as a meaning together with a useful sentence. The first activity introduces these same eight items, and spelling practice then retrieves their exact written forms.

| Item | Meaning in this lesson | Role and useful partners | Original example |
|---|---|---|---|
| bread | Food made from flour and water and usually baked | A food noun; eat bread | We eat bread. |
| rice | Small grains cooked and eaten as food | A food noun; eat rice | You eat rice. |
| water | The clear liquid people drink | A liquid noun; drink water | They drink water. |
| milk | A white liquid used as a drink or food | A liquid noun; drink milk | I drink milk. |
| eat | Put food in your mouth and swallow it | An action verb here; eat bread, eat rice | I eat bread. |
| drink | Take a liquid into your mouth and swallow it | An action verb here; drink water, drink milk | We drink milk. |
| have breakfast | Eat the first meal of the day | A complete phrase; have breakfast at home | They have breakfast at home. |
| drink water | Take water into your mouth and swallow it | An action plus its object | You drink water. |

*Drink* can be a noun in other sentences, but in **I drink milk**, it is the action. Classify a word by its job in the sentence. *Milk* remains a noun even though drinking is something we do with it.

Do not confuse an action with the thing it acts on:

- In **We eat bread**, *eat* gives the action and *bread* names the food.
- In **You drink water**, *drink* gives the action and *water* names the liquid.
- *Eat and drink* are two actions. *Bread and rice* are two foods. *Water and milk* are two liquids.

These eight items form this lesson's vocabulary review scope. Extra support words help explain the texts; they do not silently become additional intake items.

## Spelling and hearing the forms

Listen, recall the form and type it before checking. The target forms are **bread, rice, water, milk, eat, drink, have breakfast, drink water**. Keep the spaces in the two phrases. A phrase is more than one written word, even when it sounds closely connected.

The letters in **bread** do not make two separate vowel beats: it has one syllable. Listen to the recording and compare the complete word. At the ends of **drink** and **milk**, avoid losing the last sound. Speak clearly without inserting a long pause inside a word. Different accents can sound different; the aim is an understandable word, not imitation of one speaker's identity.

After word dictation, listen to prepared phrases and short sentences:

- **I have breakfast at home.** Identify *have breakfast* inside the whole sentence.
- **bread**: recognise the spoken word rather than choosing a word because it looks familiar.
- **drink water**: type both words.
- **We eat bread.** and **I drink milk.**: hear the subject, action and object together.

Replays support learning here. They do not reproduce the one-play condition of the real IELTS Listening test. Phrase recognition and dictation prepare for comprehension, but the later listening form asks the learner to understand a conversation as well.

## Building a complete statement

### Who, action, what

Use this order for the simple statements in this lesson:

**subject + action verb + object**

In **I eat rice**, the subject *I* tells us who, the verb *eat* gives the action and the object *rice* tells us what the person eats.

| Subject: who? | Verb: action? | Object: what? | Complete statement |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | eat | rice | I eat rice. |
| You | drink | water | You drink water. |
| We | eat | bread | We eat bread. |
| They | drink | milk | They drink milk. |

**I** means the person speaking. **You** means the person or people being addressed. **We** includes the speaker and at least one other person. **They** refers to other people here.

Compare:

- **I rice eat.** → **I eat rice.** Move the action before its object.
- **They milk drink.** → **They drink milk.** Keep the same three words but fix their order.
- **We bread.** → The message has a person and food but is missing the action. **We eat bread.** gives the complete message.
- **We water drink.** → **We drink water.** Transfer the pattern to a new message.

### Present simple for regular meals

**I eat rice** can describe a regular meal habit. With **I, you, we** and **they**, use **eat** and **drink** without adding *s*. Do not write **They eats bread**; write **They eat bread**. Do not insert *am* or *are* before these present-simple action verbs: **I am drink milk** becomes **I drink milk**. These focused uses follow [British Council present-simple guidance](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/present-simple).

Later, the reading text contains **Sam eats** and **He drinks**. Recognise these as the same food and drink actions with one named person or *he*. This lesson gives receptive support for those forms; the main productive pattern remains **I/you/we/they + eat/drink**. Questions and negatives are introduced only as supported listening expressions here. A later lesson develops their full grammar.

### Capital letters and full stops

Start a statement with a capital letter and finish it with a full stop. Write the pronoun **I** as a capital even when it appears inside a sentence.

- **we drink water** → **We drink water.**
- **i drink water** → **I drink water.**
- **We eat rice. I drink milk.** contains two clearly separated statements.

When checking a correction, ask: Who is the subject? Where is the action? What is its object? Does the written statement have its capital letter and full stop? Correct word order alone does not answer all four questions.

## Natural word partners and extra details

**Have breakfast** means eat the first meal of the day. Learn the complete phrase, not separate translations of *have* and *breakfast*. **Eat breakfast** is also natural. The lesson accepts both when a task asks for any natural action in **I ___ breakfast**.

**Drink water** is a direct way to name the action and liquid. **Have water** can also be natural in a meal context. It would be misleading to teach that *have water* is always wrong. In a task explicitly asking for the two action verbs *eat* and *drink*, however, **drink** is the answer for water.

**At home** tells us where: the place where someone lives. **In the morning** tells us when: the early part of the day.

- **We have breakfast at home.** Add a place after the basic message.
- **I drink milk in the morning.** Add a time after the basic message.
- **We have breakfast ___ home.** → **at**.
- **I eat bread ___ the morning.** → **in**.

In the general food meanings used here, write **bread, rice, water** and **milk** without *a* or an ordinary plural ending. **I eat bread** is the normal general statement, not **I eat a bread**. Portions, containers and different kinds require further language taught later. See [British Council guidance on countable and uncountable nouns](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/free-resources/grammar/a1-a2/nouns-countable-uncountable).

## Listening: follow each speaker's reply

The conversation uses two useful question patterns:

- **What do you eat?** asks for food.
- **Do you drink milk?** asks whether the listener drinks milk.

The question alone does not establish the answer. In **Do you eat rice? — No. I eat bread**, the reply gives *bread*. **No** rejects the suggestion. **Yes** confirms it. Listen to the full reply instead of selecting the first food word you hear.

Support language for the recording:

- **brother / sister**: a male / female sibling;
- **with me**: together with the person speaking;
- **sit**: be on a chair;
- **table**: furniture used for meals;
- **small**: not big;
- **My sister eats with me**: the speaker and their sister eat together.

Before listening, read the names and field labels. Every gap asks for one food or drink word. Keep the answer with its speaker. All four fields are submitted together so an early answer explanation does not give away later fields.

### Complete listening script

The learner hears this text during the task. The transcript appears only after the form has been answered.

**Ari:** Hello, Jo. What do you eat in the morning?

**Jo:** I eat rice.

**Ari:** Do you drink milk?

**Jo:** No. I drink water.

**Ari:** I eat bread and drink milk.

**Jo:** Do you eat at home?

**Ari:** Yes. My sister eats with me.

**Jo:** I eat with my brother. We sit at a small table.

### Form and reasoning

Instruction: **Write ONE WORD in each gap.**

| Field | Answer | Evidence and reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Jo: food | rice | Jo replies “I eat rice.” |
| Jo: drink | water | Ari asks about milk, but Jo says no and supplies water. |
| Ari: food | bread | Ari's own statement names bread. Do not transfer Jo's rice to Ari. |
| Ari: drink | milk | Ari says “drink milk”. The speaker determines whose answer it is. |

The extra details about siblings and the table make a connected conversation; they are not additional hidden questions. Before leaving the form screen, you can replay the complete dialogue if you have a play remaining (up to three total plays). Then continue to read the transcript. The practice allows replays; the real exam's word limits and recording conditions are described in the [official Listening format](https://ielts.org/take-a-test/test-types/ielts-academic-test/ielts-academic-format-listening).

## Reading: follow the people and find the stated information

The reading goal is to find who eats, what they eat, what they drink, and where or when they eat. A correct answer comes from the story. It does not come from what people usually eat in real life. The passage is a short fictional teaching text, not a timed IELTS test.

Before this exercise, the learner has met the six words **bread, rice, water, milk, eat, drink** and the two chunks **have breakfast, drink water**. They have practised the order **person + action + food or drink**. The following support words make the original passage accessible; they are support for this lesson, not additional vocabulary targets to add to Leitner.

### Times in the story

**Morning** is the early part of the day. **Lunch** is a meal in the middle of the day. **Evening** is the part of the day near the end. **Sunday** is the name of a day. Like a person's name, it starts with a capital letter.

A question may name both a person and a time. Keep both in mind. The same person may eat different food in the morning and evening. Finding the person's name alone is not enough; check the time as well.

### People, meals and places

A **brother** is a boy or man with the same parent or parents as you. A **friend** is a person you know and like. A **family** includes people such as parents, brothers and sisters. Mina, Sam and Jo are names in this story.

A **meal** is a time when you eat, such as breakfast or lunch. To **like** something means to enjoy it or feel good about it. **At home** means in the place where you live. A **table** is furniture with a flat top; food can be placed on it. **Small** and **large** describe size. Large means big. They are opposites.

### Follow words that refer to people

**I** is the person speaking or writing. **My** means belonging to that person. **We** includes the speaker or writer and another person or people. **Our** means belonging to that group. After **with**, the group can be called **us**. **Both** means the two people together. In “We both eat,” two people eat. Use the surrounding sentences to find who those people are.

Consider this separate example:

> Ali eats bread. He drinks milk.

**He** refers to Ali. The two statements describe the same person. The first sentence introduces a name; the next uses a shorter word to refer to that person. With **I** or **we**, use **eat** and **drink** in the simple statements practised so far. With **Ali** or **he**, recognise **eats** and **drinks**. The action keeps the same meaning. This lesson requires understanding those third-person forms in the reading; the learner may continue to use **I** and **we** in independent speaking and writing.

**Their table** means the table belonging to the people just mentioned. Do not interpret every new small word as a new person.

### Answer a one-word question

First identify the type of information wanted. **Who?** asks for a person. **What does someone eat or drink?** asks for food or a drink.

Separate worked example:

- Text: “My name is Ali.”
- Question: “What is the name? Write ONE WORD.”
- Answer: **Ali**.

The answer is the word itself, not “His name is Ali” and not an explanation. An explanation tells why the answer is supported; it is different from the answer entered in the box. In the reading below, return to the supporting sentence before typing. Use the word's spelling from the text.

### Complete reading text

My name is Mina. I eat bread in the morning. I drink milk. My brother, Sam, eats rice at lunch. He drinks water. We eat at home. Our table is small. In the evening, I eat rice and Sam eats bread. We both drink water. We like our meals. On Sunday, our friend Jo eats with us at home.

Read the whole message once for the people and meals. Then reread the relevant sentence for each question. The full passage remains available with the text questions; this is comprehension practice rather than a memory test of a vanished screen.

### Learn true, false and not given

The first two reading questions use one-word answers. Before the three statement judgments, learn these labels:

- **TRUE:** the text agrees with the statement.
- **FALSE:** the text says the opposite.
- **NOT GIVEN:** the text does not tell you whether the statement is true or false.

Use this separate practice text: **The table is large.**

| Statement about the practice text | Answer | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The table is large. | TRUE | The text states this. |
| The table is small. | FALSE | The text says large, the opposite of small. |
| The table is ten years old. | NOT GIVEN | The text gives its size, but no age. |

“Twenty years old” gives a person's age. When the text gives no age, do not guess it. Missing information is not evidence that an age statement is false. The large-table example is separate from Mina's story: reread Mina's text before answering its questions.

### Reading questions, keys and explanations

| Item | Question or task | Accepted answer | Explanation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| EX01-R01 | What does Mina eat in the morning? Write ONE WORD. | bread | Mina writes, “I eat bread in the morning.” Match both Mina and the morning. |
| EX01-R02 | What does Sam drink at lunch? Write ONE WORD. | water | Sam is introduced in the lunch sentence; “He drinks water” continues about Sam. Here **he** refers to Sam. |
| EX01-R03 | Mina and Sam eat at home. TRUE, FALSE or NOT GIVEN? | TRUE | “We eat at home” directly supports this. In this part of the story, we refers to Mina and Sam. |
| EX01-R04 | Their table is large. TRUE, FALSE or NOT GIVEN? | FALSE | The passage states, “Our table is small.” Small contradicts large. |
| EX01-R05 | Jo is twenty years old. TRUE, FALSE or NOT GIVEN? | NOT GIVEN | Jo is named as a friend, but the passage gives no age for Jo. |
| EX01-R06 | In “I eat bread,” identify the subject, verb and object. | I = subject; eat = verb; bread = object | I names the person doing the action. Eat is the action. Bread names what is eaten. |
| EX01-R07 | Who eats with the family on Sunday? Write ONE WORD. | Jo | The final sentence identifies Jo as the friend who eats with them on Sunday. Name the visitor, not everyone at the table. |
| EX01-R08 | What do both Mina and Sam drink in the evening? Write ONE WORD. | water | “We both drink water” follows the evening meal description. Both includes Mina and Sam. |

The reading has successfully practised its intended operation when the learner can connect an answer to the correct person, time and source sentence. Understanding an explanation after an error is useful learning; it does not retroactively turn the original error into a correct first attempt.

## Conversation: answer one question at a time

A conversation asks you to listen again after each answer. The next question may ask for a different detail. Keep the facts of your chosen meal, but respond to the question you hear now. You may describe a real or invented meal; there is no single correct personal answer.

### Notice which detail is requested

**What do you eat in the morning?** asks about food. **I eat bread** answers that question. **I eat rice** is another possible answer. The important connection is between **eat** and a food.

**What do you drink?** asks about a drink. **I drink milk** or **I drink water** answers that question. A food-only reply does not yet give the requested drink detail.

**Where do you eat breakfast?** asks about a place. **Where** means that the listener wants the place, not another food or drink. **I eat breakfast at home** gives that detail. The short reply **At home** can also communicate the place; a complete sentence gives additional practice with person, action and detail.

### Worked exchange and reasoning

| Question | One possible reply | Why the reply fits |
|---|---|---|
| What do you eat in the morning? | I eat bread. | Bread supplies the food information requested by eat. |
| What do you drink? | I drink milk. | The new question asks for a drink, so the reply adds milk. |
| Where do you eat breakfast? | I eat breakfast at home. | At home supplies the place requested by where. |

This exchange illustrates how to follow a question. It is not a script that every learner must repeat, and a later question can differ. A learner who chooses rice and water should keep those facts instead of changing them to match the examples.

Compare two replies to **Where do you eat breakfast?**:

- **I drink milk.** This is a complete grammatical statement, but it supplies a drink rather than a place.
- **I eat breakfast at home.** This supplies the requested place and keeps the breakfast topic.

Relevance and sentence form are different things. A short relevant reply can answer a question; a longer grammatical sentence can miss it. First communicate the requested detail, then practise expanding it into a clear statement.

### Listen, answer and check the meaning

Listen to the opening question, **What do you eat in the morning?**, and give your answer orally. Listen to the next question before deciding what to say. The short activity asks for two answer turns, with a maximum of three. Its 30-second recording limit is a practice setting, not an official IELTS speaking limit.

The displayed transcript represents the words the recognizer detected. Read it before interpreting feedback. If those words differ from what you said, the feedback may address a recognition error rather than your intended answer. Missing or uncertain recognition is not evidence that your English was wrong.

Text-based feedback may describe whether the recognized answer supplied the requested detail. Its narrow task-response labels distinguish **off topic**, **partly answered** and **answered**, represented by 0, 1 and 2 when a response can be assessed. Those labels do not measure overall English ability, pronunciation, fluency or an IELTS band. An unassessed response has no task score. Compare every suggestion with your intended meaning; do not invent a personal fact just to follow a suggestion.

The aim is to hear a fresh question and communicate an appropriate detail, not to memorize a fixed sequence of breakfast sentences. Later independent speaking adds a longer answer and separate self-review.

## Speaking: communicate a morning meal, then improve one thing

The speaking goal is to communicate food and drink in two or three complete statements. The learner may invent a meal. No knowledge about a particular country's breakfast is required, and there is no single correct personal meal.

### Build and hear a short model

Use **I eat + food** and **I drink + a drink**. Keep the person before the action. Two clear short statements can answer the question.

Model:

> I eat rice. I drink milk.

The first statement names the food; the second names the drink. In the app, the existing speech playback mechanism provides a generated spoken model. It is a sentence model for learning, not a natural conversation recording or an assessed listening passage.

Listen for the small pause between statements. Say the model quietly once. Notice the ending of **eat** and **drink** so that each word is clear. The purpose is intelligibility, not copying a particular accent. This exercise does not assign a pronunciation score.

### Guided recording

Prompt: **Say two statements about a meal. Use the frames to help you.**

- I eat ___.
- I drink ___.

Choose a food and a drink. The supported choices are bread, rice, water and milk. For example, “I eat bread. I drink water” is a possible response; rice and milk can also be used where appropriate. The model and frames supply substantial help, so this first recording is guided practice.

### Prepare and give the independent answer

Choose the facts for your own morning meal. You may add **in the morning** or **at home**. Think of the ideas rather than writing a script. You do not have to use every lesson word or copy the model's food and drink.

Independent prompt: **What do you eat and drink in the morning? Give two or three complete statements. You may invent a meal.**

Cover:

- what you eat;
- what you drink;
- where or when, if you want to add a third statement.

The original independent response screen no longer displays vocabulary chips or a notes field. This reduces the support compared with the guided recording. The existing 30-second preparation and 30-second recording settings remain practice controls; they are not official IELTS timing for this task.

Submit the first independent answer. Before leaving that same screen, play the submitted recording and choose one thing to improve. Reviewing it there matters: the later self-review card does not display the previous recording.

### Self-review and second recording

Use these questions:

1. Did you name a food **and** a drink?
2. Did each statement have a person and an action?
3. Could you hear each food or drink word clearly?

Choose one small change. Add a missing detail, fix word order, or make a word clearer. If the message already answers the question clearly, keep its facts and practise a more comfortable pause. Do not invent an error simply to produce a correction.

Revision prompt: **Say your morning-meal answer again with the one improvement you chose.** Keep the same meal facts, give two or three complete statements, and make the chosen change.

The guided recording, original independent answer and revised answer use separate slide IDs. The revised answer is a second practice artifact, not an independent transfer assessment after no help. Submission confirms practice evidence; it does not establish an IELTS band, accurate pronunciation or semantic mastery. The self-review is learner judgment, not automated speech evaluation.

## Writing: describe one meal, review the first version and revise

The writing goal is a short, understandable message of 20–40 words about **one meal**. It should identify who eats, what they eat and what they drink. The word range is a practice target that encourages a little development. Writing enough words alone does not demonstrate accuracy or task completion.

### Plan meaning before sentences

Choose one meal, such as breakfast. Keep the whole message about that meal. Plan three things:

- **Who?** Use **I** or **we** if those forms are easiest.
- **Food?** What do you eat?
- **Drink?** What do you drink?

You may add a time or place with **in the morning** or **at home**. Planning notes can contain a few idea words. Do not force all eight target items into a meal if they do not fit. There is no requirement to use third-person forms such as eats and drinks in this first independent message.

### Make sentences complete

Short worked example:

> We eat bread. We drink water.

Each sentence starts with the person, **We**, then an action, **eat** or **drink**, then what is eaten or drunk. Start a sentence with a capital letter and finish it with a full stop. The word **I** always has a capital letter, even when it is not the first word of a sentence.

A list such as “bread, milk, home” can help with planning, but it is not yet a complete message. Add the person and action: “I eat bread.” Give the drink its own complete statement or connect two complete statements with **and**, as in the lesson's worked meal example.

### Exact capitalization and full-stop practice

Displayed text: **i drink water**

Prompt: **Write the sentence with capital I and a final full stop.** Keep the same words. Both the capital letter and the full stop are part of this answer.

Exact answer: **I drink water.**

Why: I is always written as a capital letter. The full stop marks the end of the statement. This short-answer slide requests one brief exact sentence and uses the existing exact-spelling option to preserve case and punctuation. It does not rely on the error-correction slide's normal case-insensitive answer matching.

### First independent written version

Prompt: **Write 20–40 words about one meal. Say who eats, what they eat and what they drink. You may use I or we and invent the meal.**

Planning notes are available. The full worked model is hidden until the learner submits this first version. Vocabulary chips are omitted so the learner retrieves the taught words instead of copying a displayed list.

After Submit, read the first version while it is still visible on the response screen. Choose one useful improvement before continuing. The next card cannot display the previous response automatically. The learner carries the chosen change into the second version.

### Worked model shown after submission

> I have breakfast at home. I eat bread and I drink milk. We eat rice at breakfast. We drink water. We like our morning meal.

This is a 25-word example about one meal, breakfast. It includes who, food, drink and place. It uses I and we, clear actions, capital letters and full stops. Different facts and a different combination of the taught words can also fulfil the task. The model is not an official band exemplar, an answer to copy, or a requirement to mention all those foods and drinks.

### Review and revise your own meal message

Check:

1. Is the whole message about **one meal**?
2. Did you say **who**, **what food**, and **what drink**?
3. Does each sentence have the person before the action?
4. Did you use capital **I**, sentence capitals and full stops?

Choose one useful change. If there is no clear error, add a meaningful time or place. Keep the facts of your own meal instead of replacing them with the model's facts.

Revision prompt: **Write your revised 20–40-word message about the same meal. Keep your own facts and improve one detail, sentence, capital letter or full stop.**

The revised version has a separate response ID. It is an open written response because a learner may improve meaning or more than one local word; an exact-answer rewrite cannot fairly grade all valid meal descriptions. Keep the first independent response distinct from practice after a model and self-review.

### Use formative feedback thoughtfully

Both writing tasks provide their purpose, A1 language objectives and response expectations to the application. A valid personal answer may use a different food, drink, person or invented situation. The sample message is one example, not an exact answer key. Feedback should help the learner make one useful change while preserving their meaning and simple voice. A target of 20–40 words encourages development; length alone does not establish accuracy.

The application saves the original response before requesting optional feedback. Saving a draft, receiving feedback and demonstrating a skill are different events. If feedback is unavailable or cannot assess the response, keep the draft and use the self-review questions above. Do not interpret an unavailable service as an English mistake.

When formative feedback is available, read its quoted evidence and explanation. A suggestion may be optional, and a model can be mistaken. Check that a proposed change still says what you mean. For example, changing **I drink water** to **I drink milk** changes a personal fact; it is not a grammar correction. Selecting a suggested edit creates a separate preview. Writing a revision creates another response rather than replacing the first one. The dedicated second-version task remains supported practice even if its wording improves. Neither a short meal response nor a model's feedback is an IELTS band score.


## Recall and transfer without guessing

Several different meal choices can be perfectly good English. A closed question needs enough information to determine its answer. Compare **I eat ___**, which allows many foods, with **Which lesson food consists of small grains?**, which identifies **rice**.

The independent recall practice uses distinguishing meanings:

| Cue | Answer | Why |
|---|---|---|
| First meal of the day | breakfast | This names the meal rather than one food. |
| Food made from flour and usually baked | bread | Rice is a different food. |
| Food consisting of small grains | rice | The cue identifies the grain food. |
| Clear liquid | water | Milk is white. |
| White liquid | milk | Water is clear. |
| They ___ rice; use eat or drink | eat | Rice is the food object. |
| We ___ milk; use eat or drink | drink | Milk is the liquid object. |

Do not memorise the position of an answer on the screen. Recall its meaning, check the sentence and then retrieve its spelling.

## Final mixed review

This last exercise combines already taught language in new small messages. It introduces no new target words.

1. **Write the two-word phrase that means eat the first meal of the day.** Accept **have breakfast** or **eat breakfast**. Both express the meaning.
2. **You water drink.** Keep the same words and repair their order: **You drink water.**
3. Listen to Lee: **I have breakfast at home. I eat bread. I drink water.** Complete two one-word fields: food = **bread**; drink = **water**. The source remains spoken while answering. These are Lee's facts, not a recalled answer from Ari or Jo.
4. Read: **My name is Kim. I drink milk at breakfast. I eat rice. We eat at home.** What does Kim drink? **Milk**. The second sentence supplies the answer.

Uppercase and lowercase lexical answers are accepted in the comprehension practice. Spelling must still identify the correct word. The dedicated capitalization task is different: its exact target is **I drink water.** including the capital and full stop.

An error should lead back to the relevant teaching: a meaning error to food/liquid/action work, an order error to sentence building, a speaker error to the listening form, and an incomplete personal message to the production checklist. These are useful learner choices; completing the last screen does not by itself demonstrate mastery of everything in the lesson.

## Expected mastery and later review

The learner is ready for the next instructional step when they can:

- retrieve the eight words and phrases from meaning or sound, with correct word forms;
- construct new **I/you/we/they + eat/drink + food/liquid** statements;
- keep people and times straight when answering reading questions;
- use a speaker's reply rather than the question's suggestion as listening evidence;
- give a short original spoken answer covering food and drink;
- write and improve a short message about one meal.

Use the application's vocabulary review on later days. Revisit missed words after a gap rather than repeating only the same screen immediately. The lesson varies meaning, sound and sentence cues within one session; that does not replace review across sessions. The sequence is a Vocora teaching design informed by the existing curriculum's retrieval and spacing evidence, not an experimentally proven formula for a particular band gain.

| Order | Exercise | Why it comes here |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Vocabulary intake | Introduce all eight target forms and meanings before testing them. |

| 8 | Listening with evidence | Follow two speakers and complete one coherent form without the transcript. |
